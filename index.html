What is SAR, in plain words?

Synthetic Aperture Radar is a satellite “torch” that works at night and through cloud.

Ordinary satellite photos need sunlight and a clear sky, but the monsoon is cloudy. Sentinel-1 sends out its own microwave pulses and records how much bounces back to the satellite. That returned signal is called backscatter. It depends on how rough, wet and tall the surface is. Taking a picture every few days across a whole season (multi-temporal monitoring) lets us watch a field change from bare soil to flooded to green and tall.

📡 Backscatter (VV / VH)

VV and VH are two radar “viewing modes”. VH reacts strongly to leafy, stalky plants; VV reacts more to the ground surface.

💧 Flooded paddy

Smooth standing water acts like a mirror and bounces the beam away, so early-season paddy looks dark.

🌾 Growing crop

As rice grows, leaves and stems scatter more energy back, so the signal climbs through the season.

🕒 Change over time

Fields that stay dark or flat are likely fallow; fields that rise and fall like rice are likely active crop.

Limitations and responsible use

  • “Active vegetation” is not proof of rice. Other green crops (sugarcane, maize, fodder) and trees can look similar to radar. The map separates active growth from fallow and non-crop; it does not verify crop species.
  • Mixed pixels and small fields. A 10 m pixel can straddle field edges, bunds, roads or canals.
  • Water and moisture. Flooded fields and fresh rain change backscatter and can mimic other classes.
  • Season window. Data end 31 October 2025; late-harvested varieties may be partly missed.
